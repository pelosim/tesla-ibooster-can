Reading a Tesla brake booster over CAN, without ever talking to it

Two 500 kbps buses, fourteen message IDs, and a pedal-stroke signal calibrated to millimetres — captured passively from a Gen1 Model S booster on a bench, for a 1987 944S restomod. Plus what breaking it deliberately revealed about how it reports faults.

✓ The question that mattered

It assists standalone. 12 V, ground and ignition — nothing ever transmitted to it. No vehicle CAN, no wake frame, no keep-alive. A purely passive monitor works, so the risk of having to send commands to a brake actuator is closed rather than deferred.

01 — Pedal stroke, calibrated

Message 0x39D on the vehicle bus carries stroke as a 16-bit little-endian value in bytes 2:3, at 25 Hz. Holding at known displacements produces flat plateaus — that's what makes the fit possible. Each hold is stable to ±20 counts, about 0.06 mm.

02 — The calibration disagrees with itself

Two runs, five held positions. The fits differ by 8% in slope — and 20 mm reads higher than 21 mm, which a monotonic sensor cannot do. The signal isn't the problem: it repeats to 0.06 mm within a hold while the runs disagree by up to 4.4 mm. The ruler is the bottleneck, not the data.

03 — The two buses agree with each other

The YAW bus carries its own position signal in 0x38E bytes 3:4 at 99.7 Hz. Plotted against the calibrated vehicle-bus stroke it is a straight line — r = 0.9999 over 15,296 matched samples, with two independent runs agreeing to 0.13% on slope. That's far tighter than either agrees with the ruler, which is the independent confirmation that the measurement, not the sensor, was inconsistent.

04 — Listen-only destroys the capture

On a two-node bus a passive sniffer never acknowledges, so the booster's error counter climbs 8 per attempt, hits bus-off at ~32 ms, auto-recovers, and repeats. The result looks like a fast signal and is actually a retransmission storm — and it hides three of the four IDs. Acknowledging is a link-layer bit, not a command, so read-only capture still acknowledges.

07 — 0x33D fires after you let go

It transmits at 9.5 Hz but is all-FF 99.8% of the time. In 150 seconds it carried a real payload three times — each about 0.8 s after a brake release. Two pedal applications, two events. It is an event message, not the periodic status frame it first looked like.

08 — Fault signalling, found by breaking it

Nothing here came from watching a healthy booster. Disconnecting the travel sensor mid-run moved three signals within ~10 ms of each other — and revealed that there is no status message at all. Status is a field inside the position frames, which is why hunting for a dedicated fault frame found nothing for days.

0x39D's fault value carries a valid checksum, so it is deliberate sentinel signalling rather than corruption — and 16354 is physically unreachable, since the end stop is 13606. A consumer can reject it on range alone without knowing the sentinel exists.

09 — The fault latches, and status is not what it looks like

Reconnecting the sensor does not clear it. The booster stays in no-assist until a power cycle — and through that, the two buses disagree in a way that matters for anything consuming them.

What status 2 does NOT mean

"Position invalid." The reconnect row proves otherwise — position was live and updating while status stayed 2. Treating the nibble as a data-validity flag throws away a good reading.

What it does mean

"Assist unavailable." And during a latched fault 0x38E is the only live position source — 0x39D stays at its sentinel.

⚠️ In a car this is the part that matters. A momentary connector interruption — vibration, corrosion, a marginal crimp — latches the booster into no assist until the ignition is cycled. The pedal goes hard and stays hard, with nothing on a stock dash to explain why. The brakes still work; they just need far more effort.
