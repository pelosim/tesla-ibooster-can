What else is on the wire

01

The short answer

There is no continuous assist, force, current or pressure channel on either bus. Every byte that moves while the pedal moves is accounted for by position plus three threshold flags. But the booster does emit assist-related numbers — as a burst of six messages fired roughly a second after each brake release, containing a summary of the application that just ended.

Two of those summary fields decode cleanly and immediately: peak rod travel (r = 0.99975 against the calibrated stroke signal) and how long the brake was applied (r = 0.99877). Both are recorded to a precision better than the bench ruler that calibrated them.

Along the way, three things that were listed as unknown or constant turned out not to be: 0x38E b6 and 0x38F b2 both carry live brake flags, 0x32D is static identity data, and byte 0 of both YAW messages is a plain CRC-8 with no payload in it at all.

Why this matters for the 944. A brake-light signal already exists on the bus, twice, with hysteresis built in — which makes it a genuine candidate to cross-check the mechanical pedal switch against, even though the standing rule keeps the lights on the mechanical switch. The ~40 mm state is more interesting still: it flags the last few millimetres before the end stop.
03

The post-brake burst

Six IDs — 0x33D 0x31D 0x34D 0x36D 0x37D 0x38D — transmit one frame each, together, within about 120 ms of one another. The bench log had them down as an unexplained group with an unknown trigger. They are not unexplained: of the 12 complete bursts in the six logs, 10 follow a brake release by 0.64–2.20 seconds, and no pedal application fails to produce one.

The other two are a boot burst — one each in cal and assist, at ~6.8 s of uptime with no pedal input at all. The remaining runs started capturing too late or were mid-sweep at that moment. This page first claimed no burst occurs without a release; only the paired bursts had been looked at.

That gives ten bursts paired with ten brake applications whose peak, duration, and shape are known independently from 0x39D. Correlating every byte of the burst against every property of the application that triggered it is what the rest of this page does.

event fields × application propertiesn = 10

What tracks what

Pearson r between each burst byte and each measured property of the preceding pedal application. Uptime is included as a control — a field that only tracks uptime is a counter or a drift, not a measurement of the brake event. Known free-running counters (0x31D/0x3AD b0:b1) are excluded. With n = 10 and this many candidates, treat everything below |r| ≈ 0.9 as a lead rather than a result.

0x38D b5:b6r = 0.99975 · p < 0.0001

Peak rod travel of the application just ended

129.3 counts per millimetre. The worst residual is 0.66 mm across a 34 mm range — and the ruler that produced the reference calibration is itself only good to about ±2 mm, so this field agrees with 0x39D as closely as the calibration allows. Note the three points stacked at 42.42 mm: the mechanical end stop, hit in three separate power cycles, recorded as 5401, 5405 and 5405 — a spread of 4 counts, or 0.03 mm.

0x37D b0:b1r = 0.99877 · p < 0.0001

How long the brake was held

A 16-bit tick count, linear in application duration over a 0.8 s to 49 s range, at 27.5 ticks per second. That rate is not a round number, which means either the tick is ~36 ms or the booster's own start/stop threshold differs slightly from the 600-count threshold used here — fitting against thresholds from 400 to 1000 counts moves the slope by less than 0.2%, so the threshold is low and the tick is the open question.

0x31D b2 high nibbler = 0.884 · p < 0.0001

The same duration, bucketed on a log scale

A 4-bit field taking values 1–7, monotonic in duration with roughly one step per doubling. A logarithmic bucket index is what diagnostic event memory looks like, not what a control signal looks like — which is a strong hint about what this whole burst is for. Bucket edges cannot be pinned down from ten events.

0x33D b4:b7Unresolved

Four channels of something, tightly clustered

All four panels share one vertical scale. These are four bytes that always sit within a few counts of each other, in the range 76–90, all drifting upward with peak travel (r = 0.53 to 0.92) but far too compressed to be a position. Four near-identical channels of one quantity is the shape of a redundant sensor set or a set of sampled supply readings. Distinguishing those needs a run with the bench supply deliberately varied — nothing in these captures separates them.

04

Three corrections to the existing decode

0x38E b0 · 0x38F b0Confirmed · 70,438 frames

Byte 0 is a CRC-8, and contains no data

Both YAW messages protect bytes 1–7 with CRC-8/SAE-J1850 — polynomial 0x1D, init 0x00, final XOR 0x0A, no reflection. It validates on 100% of 46,960 0x38E frames and 23,478 0x38F frames across four power cycles, with the same parameters for both IDs — so the CRC is not seeded from the message ID. This closes the question of whether anything was hiding in a byte that looked random. Nothing was. The vehicle bus does not share the scheme: 0x39D keeps its additive (b1+b2+b3+0xA0) checksum, and none of the burst IDs carry a checksum at all, which is why their byte 0 is free to be data.

0x32DConfirmed · 4 power cycles

Static identity data, multiplexed on byte 0

Not "structured; identity or config?" — settled. Five frames cycle on a 2-second rotation, selected by b0 ∈ {0A 0B 0D 14 16}, and each one is byte-for-byte identical across all four independent power cycles. Nothing in it responds to the pedal. It is a part number, a calibration ID, or similar, and a monitor can read it once at boot and ignore the message thereafter.

0x31D b0:b1 · 0x3AD b0:b1Confirmed · 4 runs

A shared uptime counter — and a correlation trap

A 16-bit little-endian counter running at 9.99 ticks per second — a 100 ms tick — measured across four runs including one where the capture started long after the booster was powered. Both IDs carry the same counter, offset only by their position in the burst. It matters because it correlates with almost everything: later events in a run had longer, deeper pedal applications, so an uptime counter fakes a relationship with peak, duration and work done. It was excluded from the matrix above for that reason, and it is the reason uptime is plotted there as a control column.

06

What to capture next

Everything above came from logs recorded for a different purpose — pedal sweeps for calibration. Three of the open questions would close with one deliberate session:

  • Many short, varied brake applications. Twenty or thirty events instead of ten — varying depth, speed and hold time independently — would separate the burst fields that track peak from those that track rate or work done. Right now peak and duration are partly confounded because a deep application was usually also a long one.
  • A supply-voltage sweep. Run the bench supply from 11 V to 14 V while braking identically. If 0x33D b4:b7 moves with voltage, those four channels are settled in one test.
  • A soak. Leave it powered and idle for twenty minutes. 0x34D b5 steps 0x26 → 0x28 → 0x2A monotonically with uptime across these runs, which is what a slowly warming temperature looks like — but uptime and cumulative braking are confounded in every log here, and a soak with no pedal input separates them completely.

None of it changes the monitor design. The signals worth putting on the dash — position, status, and now a brake flag and an end-of-travel flag — are all continuous, all confirmed, and all on the 100 Hz YAW bus.
